# Kohaku · after Spirited Away — research

*Kohaku* is the river Haku was, and the name Chihiro gives back to him: Nigihayami Kohakunushi, the spirit of the
Kohaku River, which was filled in and built over. The show is named for that name, because the film turns on it.

## The film

*Spirited Away* (千と千尋の神隠し, *Sen to Chihiro no Kamikakushi*), written and directed by Hayao Miyazaki, Studio
Ghibli, released in Japan on 20 July 2001. Music by Joe Hisaishi. It won the Golden Bear at Berlin (2002, shared) and
the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature (2003).

Chihiro, ten, sulking in the back of the car on the way to a new town, goes with her parents through a tunnel in a red
gate into an empty town of eating houses across a river of stones. At dusk the lanterns come on, the spirits come, her
parents have become pigs, and a boy, Haku, takes her over the bridge to the bathhouse. To stay she must work, and to
work she must give her name to Yubaba, who keeps it: she becomes Sen. She cleans the stink spirit no one will touch, who
turns out to be a great river's spirit choked with what people threw into it. She lets No-Face in out of the rain. She
sees a white dragon chased over the sea by paper birds, and knows him for Haku; he comes in through her room's window,
torn and bleeding, and she follows him, saves him, and goes by the train over the flooded plain to the Sixth Station,
Swamp Bottom, to give back what he stole. Coming home on his back she remembers falling into a river when she was
small, and the river carrying her to the shallows: it was the Kohaku, and he was it. When she says his name his scales
come off him and they fall together through the sky, hand in hand. In the morning he walks her to the edge of the field
and tells her to go and not look back. She doesn't.

## What the show keeps, and what it leaves

The bar is a short film: the iconic beats with scale, duration and clarity, in a locked cast with one job per prop.
Four minutes of music will not hold the whole film, so it keeps the spine (she comes in, she works, he is hurt, she goes
over the water for him, she gives him his name, she goes home) and the images the film is remembered by:

- **Kept:**
  - the red gate and its tunnel, and the wind going into it;
  - the summer meadow and its towering cloud;
  - the river of stones, crossed going in and again going home;
  - the empty town, its lanterns coming on at dusk;
  - the bridge, and Haku on it;
  - the bathhouse lit at night, storey on storey, its chimney;
  - the river spirit in the great bath (the thorn pulled, the junk pouring out, the clean old face rising and going);
  - the plain become a sea at dawn;
  - the white dragon and the paper birds;
  - the shutter;
  - the train over the water, its shadow passengers, the girl left on the platform;
  - Swamp Bottom's hopping lamp;
  - the flight under the moon, and the scales;
  - the fall;
  - "don't look back".
- **Left out:**
  - the parents and the pigs, Yubaba, Zeniba and Boh: each needs a scene to mean anything;
  - the contract and the stolen name: told instead by the name given back;
  - No-Face's gluttony: he is only what he is on the train, a quiet companion;
  - the dumpling, the seal and the slug: Haku is hurt, and that is all the show needs.
- **Changed:**
  - the dragon comes in through her door, not a window, and a shutter drops on the birds: in a section through her room
    that is a thing that can be seen;
  - she remembers by seeing a river through a gap in the cloud below them, not in a flashback;
  - they come down on the river of stones, where he can go no further.

## The look

Miyazaki's world is painted, not drawn: soft gradients of sky, heaped summer cumulus with flat shaded undersides,
greens in many layers, the bathhouse's vermilion and copper green against night blue. The show flattens that into the
house style the other movie shows use: flat fills, a few gradients where light falls across something, ink only on made
things, depth from layers paling into the air, and light only where it comes from (a lantern's pool, the day in at a
door). Nothing glows. Every person is a ball; everything else is drawn.

## The music

Joe Hisaishi's score for *Spirited Away* was recorded with the New Japan Philharmonic; its soundtrack album (Tokuma
Japan Communications / Studio Ghibli Records, 11 July 2001) opens with **"One Summer's Day"** (あの夏へ, *Ano Natsu e*,
3:09). The theme comes back at the film's end ("The Return"), and Hisaishi later set words to it as **"The Name of
Life"** (いのちの名前, *Inochi no Namae*, sung by Ayaka Hirahara), which is why it suits a show about a name.

The show plays Hisaishi's own later recording with the **London Symphony Orchestra** from *Melodyphony* (2010),
reissued on *Dream Songs: The Essential Joe Hisaishi* (Decca Gold), from the official music video on his own channel.
It runs 4:05 and has the shape of a whole short film: four soft chords on the piano, the theme alone, two climbs to
the same cadence through A-flat and B-flat, a deep quiet, and the theme a last time to the fullest climax, then a long
fall away. The cue's measured structure is in `cue.md`.

Two other cues were weighed and left: "The Sixth Station" (the train's own, as Hisaishi plays it solo on *Encore*,
2002) is restrained and right for the train but has no climb for the dragon; "Reprise" (the flight home, as the Royal
Philharmonic plays it on *A Symphonic Celebration*, 2023) is loud from its first bar and has no quiet for the tunnel
and the town.
